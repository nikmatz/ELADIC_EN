Linear Vector Spaces

Chapter 1 · Linear Vector Spaces

How to use them. The exercises are arranged by section of the chapter and, within each group, in increasing order of difficulty: BASIC INTERMEDIATE ADVANCED.

All of them are solved using only the tools of Chapter 1 and Chapter 0: definitions, linear combinations and the inner product. In every question about structure the answer is never a plain “yes” or “no”: if the set has the required property, a full verification of the corresponding conditions is needed; if it does not, one explicit counterexample suffices. Every computational result can also be checked on your own: the coordinates by substituting into the linear combination, the orthonormality by the inner products, the projection by checking that the residual is orthogonal to the subspace. You do not need a solutions manual.

A. Vector space axioms

Check the axioms one by one; a single counterexample is enough to make the structure collapse.

1E.1 BASIC

On the set $\mathbb{R}^2$ we define the operations $(x_1,x_2)\oplus(y_1,y_2)=(x_1+y_1,\;x_2+y_2+1)$ and $\lambda\odot(x_1,x_2)=(\lambda x_1,\;\lambda x_2)$. (α) Show that $\oplus$ is commutative and associative and determine the identity element as well as the negative of each vector. (β) Examine whether $(\mathbb{R}^2,\oplus,\odot)$ is a vector space and, if not, identify explicitly an axiom that is violated.

Hint: do not take for granted that the identity element is $(0,0)$; find it from its definition.

1E.2 BASIC

On the set $V=(0,+\infty)$ we define $x\oplus y=xy$ and $\lambda\odot x=x^{\lambda}$, for $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$. Show that $(V,\oplus,\odot)$ is a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$, determining the identity element and the negative of an $x\in V$. Then find a basis and its dimension.

Hint: every axiom reduces to a familiar property of powers.

1E.3 INTERMEDIATE

Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$. Prove, using only the axioms, that (α) $\lambda\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}$ for every $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ and (β) if $\lambda\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$, then $\lambda=0$ or $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$.

Hint: in (β) distinguish the cases $\lambda=0$ and $\lambda\neq0$; in the second you have $\lambda^{-1}$.

B. Subspaces

Three checks: the zero vector belongs, closure under addition, closure under scalar multiplication.

1E.4 BASIC

Examine, justifying fully, which of the following subsets of $\mathbb{R}^3$ are subspaces: (α) $U_1=\{(x,y,z):x-2y+3z=0\}$· (β) $U_2=\{(x,y,z):x\geq 0\}$· (γ) $U_3=\{(x,y,z):x=2z\ \text{and}\ y=-z\}$. For those that are subspaces, give a basis and their dimension.

1E.5 INTERMEDIATE

In the space $\mathcal{C}[0,1]$ of continuous functions examine which of the following sets are subspaces: (α) $A=\{f:\,f(0)=f(1)\}$· (β) $B=\{f:\,f(0)=1\}$· (γ) $\Gamma=\{f:\,f(x)\geq 0\ \text{for every}\ x\in[0,1]\}$· (δ) $\Delta=\{f:\,f(\tfrac12)=0\}$.

1E.6 ADVANCED

Let $V$ be a vector space and $S$ a nonempty subset of $V$. Prove that $\operatorname{span}(S)$ is a subspace of $V$ and that it is contained in every subspace $W$ with $S\subseteq W$; that is, that it is the smallest subspace containing $S$.

Hint: for the second part start from an arbitrary linear combination of elements of $S$ and use the closure of $W$.

C. Linear independence

You always start from the relation $c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_k\mathbf{v}_k=\mathbf{0}$ and ask whether it forces $c_1=\cdots=c_k=0$.

1E.7 BASIC

Examine whether the vectors $\mathbf{v}_1=(1,-1,2)$, $\mathbf{v}_2=(2,0,1)$, $\mathbf{v}_3=(0,-2,3)$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$ are linearly independent and, if they are not, write explicitly a dependence relation among them.

1E.8 BASIC

Prove that two vectors $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}$ of a vector space are linearly dependent if and only if at least one of them is a scalar multiple of the other. Apply the criterion to $(2,-4,6)$ and $(-3,6,-9)$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Hint: be careful in the case where one of the two vectors is the zero vector.

1E.9 INTERMEDIATE

Examine whether the functions $f_1(t)=e^{t}$, $f_2(t)=e^{2t}$, $f_3(t)=e^{3t}$ are linearly independent as elements of the space $\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})$ of continuous functions.

Hint: with the substitution $x=e^{t}$ the relation $c_1f_1+c_2f_2+c_3f_3=0$ becomes a polynomial identity in $x>0$; when does a polynomial vanish identically?

D. Basis and dimension

A basis means linear independence and spanning at the same time; the dimension is the number of its elements.

1E.10 BASIC

Let $W=\{p\in\mathcal{P}_3:\ p(-t)=p(t)\ \text{for every}\ t\in\mathbb{R}\}$. Show that $W$ is a subspace of $\mathcal{P}_3$ and find a basis and its dimension.

Hint: write $p(t)=a_0+a_1t+a_2t^2+a_3t^3$ and see what the condition imposes on the coefficients.

1E.11 BASIC

Show that the set $\mathcal{B}=\{1,\;1+t,\;1+t+t^2\}$ is a basis of $\mathcal{P}_2$ and find the coordinates with respect to $\mathcal{B}$ (α) of $p(t)=t^2$ and (β) of the general polynomial $p(t)=a_0+a_1t+a_2t^2$.

1E.12 INTERMEDIATE

In $\mathbb{R}^4$ we are given $\mathbf{v}_1=(1,1,0,1)$, $\mathbf{v}_2=(2,1,1,0)$, $\mathbf{v}_3=(0,1,-1,2)$ and $W=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{v}_3\}$. Find a basis and the dimension of $W$ and then extend this basis to a basis of the whole of $\mathbb{R}^4$.

Hint: check first whether one of the three vectors can be written as a linear combination of the other two.

E. Sum of subspaces

Grassmann's formula $\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)$ is the central tool; the sum is direct exactly when the intersection is trivial.

1E.13 BASIC

In $\mathbb{R}^4$ we are given $U=\operatorname{span}\{(1,0,0,0),(0,1,0,0)\}$ and $W=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,0,0),(0,0,1,0)\}$. Find $U+W$ and $U\cap W$, verify Grassmann's formula and examine whether the sum is direct.

1E.14 INTERMEDIATE

In $\mathcal{M}_{2\times2}(\mathbb{R})$ consider the set $\mathcal{S}$ of the symmetric matrices, that is, those with $a_{12}=a_{21}$, and the set $\mathcal{A}$ of the skew-symmetric ones, that is, those with $a_{11}=a_{22}=0$ and $a_{12}=-a_{21}$. (α) Show that $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{A}$ are subspaces and find a basis and the dimension of each. (β) Prove that $\mathcal{M}_{2\times2}(\mathbb{R})=\mathcal{S}\oplus\mathcal{A}$ and decompose into its two components the matrix $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&5\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$.

Hint: for the intersection, ask yourself which matrix is simultaneously symmetric and skew-symmetric.

1E.15 ADVANCED

Let $V$ be a vector space with $\dim V=7$ and $U,W$ subspaces of it with $\dim U=4$ and $\dim W=5$. Prove that $\dim(U\cap W)\geq 2$. Generalize: if $\dim V=n$, $\dim U=p$ and $\dim W=q$, what is the smallest possible $\dim(U\cap W)$?

Hint: combine Grassmann's formula with the restriction $\dim(U+W)\leq\dim V$.

F. Inner product and orthogonality

The inner product measures lengths and angles; orthogonality simplifies drastically every computation of coordinates.

1E.16 BASIC

We are given $\mathbf{u}=(1,2,-2)$ and $\mathbf{v}=(3,0,4)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Compute $\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle$, the lengths $\|\mathbf{u}\|$, $\|\mathbf{v}\|$ and the cosine of their angle. Then decompose $\mathbf{u}$ into a component parallel and a component perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$ and verify that the two components are indeed orthogonal.

1E.17 INTERMEDIATE

Show that $\mathbf{w}_1=(1,2,1)$, $\mathbf{w}_2=(1,-1,1)$, $\mathbf{w}_3=(1,0,-1)$ form an orthogonal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$, orthonormalize them and write $\mathbf{b}=(3,1,2)$ as a linear combination of them using the formula for the coordinates with respect to an orthogonal basis.

Hint: in an orthogonal basis the coefficient of $\mathbf{w}_i$ comes from a single inner product, without solving a system.

1E.18 INTERMEDIATE

Examine whether the map $\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=2x_1y_1+x_1y_2+x_2y_1+x_2y_2$, where $\mathbf{x}=(x_1,x_2)$ and $\mathbf{y}=(y_1,y_2)$, defines an inner product on $\mathbb{R}^2$. If it does, compute with it the norm of $(1,-1)$ and examine whether $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are orthogonal.

Hint: the critical axiom is positive definiteness; complete the square in the expression $\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle$.

G. The Gram–Schmidt process and the orthogonal complement

Orthonormalization, orthogonal projection and decomposition with respect to the orthogonal complement are three aspects of the same idea.

1E.19 BASIC

Apply the Gram–Schmidt process to $\mathbf{v}_1=(1,1,1)$ and $\mathbf{v}_2=(1,1,0)$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$ and find an orthonormal basis $\{\mathbf{q}_1,\mathbf{q}_2\}$ of the plane $W=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2\}$. Verify that $\langle\mathbf{q}_1,\mathbf{q}_2\rangle=0$ and $\|\mathbf{q}_1\|=\|\mathbf{q}_2\|=1$.

1E.20 INTERMEDIATE

With the basis $\{\mathbf{q}_1,\mathbf{q}_2\}$ of the previous exercise, find the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{b}=(2,1,3)$ onto the subspace $W$, the residual $\mathbf{b}-P_W(\mathbf{b})$ and the distance of $\mathbf{b}$ from $W$. Verify that the residual is orthogonal to both vectors that span $W$.

1E.21 INTERMEDIATE

In $\mathbb{R}^4$ we are given $W=\{(x,y,z,w):\ x+y+z+w=0\ \text{and}\ x-y+z-w=0\}$. (α) Find a basis and the dimension of $W$. (β) Determine the orthogonal complement $W^{\perp}$ and verify that $\dim W+\dim W^{\perp}=4$. (γ) Decompose $\mathbf{b}=(4,1,2,3)$ in the form $\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{w}^{\perp}$ with $\mathbf{w}\in W$ and $\mathbf{w}^{\perp}\in W^{\perp}$.

Hint: the basis you will find for $W$ in (α) is already orthogonal; use this in (γ).

1E.22 ADVANCED

Let $\{\mathbf{q}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{q}_k\}$ be an orthonormal basis of a subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Prove that for every $\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ we have $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{q}_i\rangle^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}$ and that equality holds if and only if $\mathbf{v}\in W$.

Hint: apply the Pythagorean theorem to the decomposition $\mathbf{v}=P_W(\mathbf{v})+\bigl(\mathbf{v}-P_W(\mathbf{v})\bigr)$.

H. Mixed and composite problems

Here several concepts of the chapter are combined: first recognize the structure and then choose your tool.

1E.23 INTERMEDIATE

In $\mathbb{R}^3$ consider the set $\mathcal{B}=\{(1,1,0),\,(0,1,1),\,(1,0,1)\}$. (α) Show that it is a basis. (β) Find the coordinates of $\mathbf{v}=(4,5,3)$ with respect to $\mathcal{B}$ and verify the result by substitution. (γ) Examine whether $\mathcal{B}$ is an orthogonal basis and explain why the formula for the coordinates via inner products does not apply here.

1E.24 ADVANCED

In $\mathcal{P}_2$ we define $\langle p,q\rangle=p(-1)q(-1)+p(0)q(0)+p(1)q(1)$. (α) Prove that this is an inner product. (β) Show that $r_0(t)=1$, $r_1(t)=t$ and $r_2(t)=t^2-\tfrac{2}{3}$ form an orthogonal basis of $\mathcal{P}_2$ with respect to it. (γ) Write $p(t)=t^2+t+1$ as a linear combination of $r_0,r_1,r_2$.

Hint: for the positive definiteness in (α), think how many roots a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $2$.

Exercises for the section

1E.25

Examine whether $V=\{(x,y)\in\R^2 : x+y=1\}$ with the usual operations is a vector space. Justify fully.

1E.26

Examine whether $V = \R^2$ with $\vecv \oplus \vecw = \vecv - \vecw$ and $\lambda \odot \vecv = \lambda\vecv$ is a vector space.

1E.27

Which of the following sets is a vector space? (α) $\{p \in \mathcal{P}_2 : p(0)=1\}$, (β) $\{p \in \mathcal{P}_2 : p(0)=0\}$.

1E.28

Prove that $(-1)\vecv = -\vecv$ for every $\vecv$ in a vector space.

1E.29

Which of the following are subspaces of $\R^3$? (α) $W_1=\{(t,2t,3t):t\in\R\}$, (β) $W_2=\{(x,y,z):x^2+y^2=z\}$, (γ) $W_3=\{(x,y,z):x+y=0,\,y+z=0\}$.

1E.30

Examine whether $W=\{(x,y)\in\R^2 : xy=0\}$ is a subspace. Justify your answer.

1E.31

Examine whether $\{(1,2,3),(0,1,2),(1,0,-1)\}$ are linearly independent.

1E.32

Find the values of $\lambda$ for which $\{(1,0,0),(0,1,\lambda),(\lambda,1,0)\}$ are linearly dependent.

1E.33

Prove that if $\{\vecu,\vecv\}$ are linearly independent, then $\{\vecu+\vecv,\vecu-\vecv\}$ are linearly independent as well.

1E.34

Examine whether $\{t+1,\ t^2-1,\ t^2+t\}$ are linearly independent in $\mathcal{P}_2$.

1E.35

Find a basis and the dimension of $W=\{(x,y,z,w)\in\R^4:x+y=0,\;z-w=0\}$.

1E.36

What is the dimension of $\mathcal{M}_{2\times3}(\R)$? Give a basis.

1E.37

Find a basis and the dimension $\dim W$ of $W=\{(x,y,z):x-y+2z=0,\;2x+y-z=0\}$.

1E.38

(General – engineering) Show that two non-parallel forces of the plane form a basis of $\R^2$. Decompose the force $\vecF=(5,3)$ with respect to the basis $\{(2,1),(1,1)\}$ and confirm that the components are unique.

1E.39

If $V=W_1\oplus W_2$ with $\dim V=n$ and $\dim W_1=k$, find $\dim W_2$. Justify your answer using the direct sum theorem.

1E.40

Let $V = \R^2$ with $\vecx \cdot \vecy = (x_1 y_1,\, 0)$ for $\vecx=(x_1,x_2)$, $\vecy=(y_1,y_2)$. Examine whether this defines an algebra.

1E.41

Compute $\langle \vecu,\vecv\rangle$, $\|\vecu\|$, $\|\vecv\|$ and the angle between $\vecu=(2,-1,3)$ and $\vecv=(1,2,1)$.

Cross Product and Scalar Triple Product

1E.42

Compute $\vecu\times\vecv$ for $\vecu=(1,2,3)$, $\vecv=(4,-1,2)$ and verify that $(\vecu\times\vecv)\perp\vecu$ and $(\vecu\times\vecv)\perp\vecv$.

1E.43

Examine whether $\vecu=(1,2,3)$, $\vecv=(2,4,6)$, $\vecw=(1,0,-1)$ are coplanar. Justify your answer using the scalar triple product.

Exercises for the section

1E.44

Consider the plane $\Pi: x-2y+2z=5$. (α) Find the distance of $P(1,1,1)$ from it. (β) Find the foot of the perpendicular, that is, the projection of $P$ onto $\Pi$.

1E.45

Find the parametric equation of the line of intersection of the planes $x+y+z=1$ and $2x-y+3z=4$. (Hint: $\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{n}_1\times\mathbf{n}_2$.)

1E.46

Compute the distance of the point $P(1,2,3)$ from the line $\mathbf{p}(t)=(1,0,0)+t(0,1,1)$, in two ways: (α) by projection, (β) by the cross-product formula. Verify that they agree.

1E.47

Find the angle between the planes $2x+y-z=3$ and $x-y+2z=1$ and check whether either of the lines $\mathbf{d}=(1,1,1)$ or $\mathbf{d}=(1,-3,-1)$ is parallel to the first one.

Orthogonality and Gram–Schmidt

1E.48

Check whether $\{(1,1,0),(1,-1,0),(0,0,2)\}$ form an orthogonal set and orthonormalize it.

1E.49

Apply Gram–Schmidt to $\{1,t,t^2\}\subset\mathcal{C}[-1,1]$ with $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)g(t)\,dt$.

1E.50

Prove that in the $QR$ factorization the matrix $R$ is necessarily upper triangular. (Hint: each $\veca_j$ is written only in terms of $\vecq_1,\ldots,\vecq_j$.)

Orthogonal Complement and Least Squares

1E.51

Find the orthogonal complement $W^\perp$ of $W=\{(x,y,z):x+y+z=0\}$ in $\R^3$.

1E.52

Find the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{b}=(1,2,3,4)^\top$ onto the subspace $W=\Span\{(1,1,0,0),(0,0,1,1)\}$ of $\R^4$ and verify that the residual $\mathbf{b}-\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ is orthogonal to $W$.

1E.53

Let $W$ be a subspace of $\R^n$ and $P=A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$ the matrix of orthogonal projection onto $W$ (the columns of $A$ form a basis of $W$). Prove that $I-P$ is the matrix of orthogonal projection onto $W^\perp$ and that $P(I-P)=O$.

Composite Problems

1E.54

Show that $\vecu_1=(1,1,1,1)$, $\vecu_2=(1,1,-1,-1)$, $\vecu_3=(1,-1,1,-1)$ are pairwise orthogonal, orthonormalize them and complete them to an orthonormal basis of $\R^4$.

Orthogonality and Gram – Schmidt

1E.55

Solve the least squares problem for $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$, $\vecb=(1,2,2)^{T}$ twice: (α) with the normal equations $A^{T}A\hat{\vecx}=A^{T}\vecb$, (β) via $QR$ solving $R\hat{\vecx}=Q^{T}\vecb$. Compare the amount of computation.
